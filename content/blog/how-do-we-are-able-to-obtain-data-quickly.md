The Internet is a sophisticated, interconnected network of networks that allows computers and other devices all over the world to communicate. The World Wide Web is one of the many services built on top of it. Humanity as a whole has been propelled to new heights. Innovations, ideas, and culture that were once restricted by geography now flow freely across the digital world. So HOW did we make information so easily accessible and quick to obtain?

The first component that contributes to the speedy nature of information exchange is the ingenious use of protocols, namely TCP and UDP. When we connect to a website, for example, most traditional web connections rely on TCP, while newer technologies such as HTTP/3 use QUIC, which operates over UDP. Thanks to how much connectivity has improved over the last decade, data can now be accessed almost instantly! But how do these protocols work? The TCP protocol guides our computer through a process known as a "three-way handshake" to establish a connection with the host server. Once connected, TCP ensures that data is delivered reliably and in the correct order by detecting lost packets and retransmitting them when necessary. Conversely, UDP allows machines to send information without first establishing a connection or waiting for built-in delivery confirmations. This reduces overhead and can minimize delays, making it particularly useful for real-time applications such as online gaming and video calls. However, this results in a faster but potentially less reliable method of communication, right? Actually, although UDP itself does not guarantee delivery, applications can compensate for missing information through techniques such as error concealment or selective retransmission. These techniques help create a near-perfect illusion of real-time communication, even when some data is lost along the way.

Of course, we cannot just credit smart protocols alone for how fast our data flows. Optimization is another key aspect of making the system function efficiently. The Internet consists of numerous interconnected networks, including those operated by ISPs (Internet Service Providers), which connect individual devices to the wider Internet. When information travels between these networks, routers determine where packets should be forwarded to reach their destinations. Routing protocols allow these networks to exchange information about available paths, helping data travel efficiently across the Internet. Furthermore, technologies such as Content Delivery Networks (CDNs) store copies of frequently accessed data on servers closer to users, significantly reducing the distance that information needs to travel. That opens up the ability to optimize data transmission on both a local and global scale, which ultimately contributes to how fast the whole system can be!

Last but not least, the physical layer – how do we physically connect the world together? The invention and widespread adoption of fiber-optic cables marked a turning point and became one of the keys to unlocking the potential of near-instant communication. Gone are the days when long-distance data transmission depended primarily on electrical signals traveling through copper cables. Now, thanks to fiber-optic technology, our data travels as pulses of light through glass fibers at approximately two-thirds of the speed of light in a vacuum. More importantly, fiber-optic cables can carry enormous amounts of information over long distances with relatively little signal loss, allowing modern networks to achieve incredibly high bandwidth. However, physically extending fiber-optic infrastructure still remains a difficult task due to geography, construction costs, and geopolitics. Places with access to advanced infrastructure enjoy fast connections, while those without it still suffer from subpar speeds.
